**Chesterton's fence** _n._ The principle that you should not remove or change something until you understand why it exists. From a 1929 essay by G.K. Chesterton: if you come across a fence in the middle of a road and can't see why it's there, you shouldn't remove it until you can — because whoever built it had a reason, and until you know the reason you don't know what you're removing. In software, this applies to legacy code, seemingly redundant checks, unusual configuration, and inherited processes that nobody can explain. The instinct of new engineers is often to clean up anything that looks unnecessary; Chesterton's fence is the counterargument that apparent unnecessariness is evidence of ignorance, not evidence of waste. The fence often encodes a hard-won lesson about an edge case, a security constraint, or a regulatory requirement. Delete it and you may get the production incident that explains why it was there. #AI-Gen
